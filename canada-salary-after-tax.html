Which province keeps the most?

It depends on your pay, and the gaps are smaller than most people expect. Between about $49,000 and about $148,000, British Columbia keeps the most. Above that, Alberta does. Ontario keeps the least at every pay level in the table above. At $60,000 the three provinces are only $415 a year apart.

At $100,000, British Columbia leaves you $1,167 more a year than Ontario and $915 more than Alberta. At $200,000, Alberta leaves you $6,575 more than Ontario and $1,579 more than British Columbia.

This compares take-home pay only. Housing, sales tax, insurance and everything else that differs between provinces is not in these numbers.

Why Ontario takes more than its rates suggest

Ontario adds two things on top of its tax rates. First, a surtax: 20% extra on Ontario tax above $5,818 and 36% above $7,446. On our numbers it starts to apply at about $98,600 of pay. Second, a health premium of up to $900 a year.

At $100,000 of pay, the next $1,000 you earn costs you about $315 in tax in Ontario, $282 in British Columbia and $305 in Alberta.

CPP and EI: where they stop

CPP takes 5.95% of pay above $3,500, up to $74,600 of pay (at most $4,230.45 a year). CPP2 adds 4% on pay from $74,600 to $85,000 (at most $416.00). EI takes 1.63% of pay up to $68,900 (at most $1,123.07).

So from $85,000 up, CPP, CPP2 and EI together stay at $5,769.52 whether you earn $85,000 or $500,000. Only income tax keeps growing. CPP is not lost money: it builds your own pension.

What these numbers leave out

RRSP contributions, employer benefits and pensions, credits for dependants or age, and tax in Quebec, which has its own system and is not covered. Alberta’s basic personal amount and credit rate come from published reports rather than an official table, as the methodology page notes. Sources and dates for every rule are on our methodology page. These are estimates, not tax advice.
